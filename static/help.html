What it does

Cue Media Engine plays your audio files through the computer's output (for example a Raspberry Pi feeding a mixer) and lets Bitfocus Companion buttons control it. You build playlists here, then fire them from a Stream Deck or from this page.

Getting started

  1. Add audio. Drag files and folders together anywhere onto the Library, or press the orange upload button beside the search box and choose Files or Folder. A folder becomes a new folder in the library (folders inside it are kept), and only audio files go in. Drop it onto an existing folder to put it inside that folder, or onto a playlist to add its tracks as well. Each file shows a progress bar in the library while it uploads. WAV, MP3, OGG and FLAC all work.
  2. Make a playlist. Type a name under Playlists and press Create. Or drag a track onto the empty playlist area and one is made for you.
  3. Add tracks. Drag them from the Library into a playlist, or use the + next to a file.
  4. Go. Press the green Go button on a track to play it.

Trigger points

A trigger point makes the player do a Play on Top by itself when the live track reaches a moment you chose: for example a voice-over at 0:10 of a long music bed. It uses the same ducking as the Play on Top button.

  • Open the trim window (the bracket icon on a row), find Trigger points, move the play position and press Add trigger at playhead. Choose the library track to play, or drag the purple flag on the waveform. Test plays that track on the preview output. Save keeps the triggers with the trim; Cancel discards them.
  • First pass only stops a looping track from firing the trigger again on every repeat.
  • On the Live panel a line shows Next trigger in 0:42 → name with a Skip button (skips just that one, once). Flags on the live waveform turn grey once they have fired. A short notice appears when a trigger fires, is skipped or fails.
  • The Auto triggers switch beside the Live heading turns every trigger off (it turns red and says AUTO TRIGGERS OFF). Use it when you do not want anything to play by itself.
  • A trigger does not fire when you seek past it (seeking back arms it again), while paused, if a Play on Top is already playing (a notice says so), if the track is no longer the live one, or if its time is outside the trimmed part.
  • If the target file leaves the library the trigger stays, with a warning, and nothing plays.

Triggers are not available from Companion: no variables or actions.

Crossfade time for one track

The master crossfade time is in Settings. For a particular track, type a time in seconds in the small box under the cut / crossfade toggle beside Go (it acts on the cued track and is dimmed while Go is set to cut); that track then crossfades in over its own time instead, and its row shows an XF badge. Leave the box empty to use the master time.

Start, end and fade-out of one track (A and B)

The bracket icon on a row opens the Set A and B window. Hover the waveform (or drag a marker) and a bubble shows the time under the pointer and the time left in the file; Playhead beside “Plays… of…” is the play position. At B is a Fade out or a Cut. With Fade out, for 1.5 s sets how long the fade lasts (0.1 to 10 s: type it, use the small arrows in the field, or press Up / Down while it is selected). Until you change it the track uses the default from Settings → Audio → Transitions → Fade-out at B and shows “(default)”; Use default goes back to it. The fade can never be longer than the part between A and B. The red wedge on the waveform is the fade, and the Preview buttons play it with the length shown, even before you Save.

At A is Cut (the track starts at full level, as always) or Fade in, with its own length in the same way (the green wedge after A; the default is Settings → Audio → Transitions → Fade-in at A, 0 = none). The fade-in plays when the track starts at A; it is not used when you seek into the track or resume it part-way in. A crossfade into the track is its fade-in (the crossfade wins). A track played on top fades up to its set level while the background ducks at full depth.

Precision (the sliders button, blue when on, remembered on this device) shows a time field for A and B with up / down arrows (hold to repeat, Shift = 10×) and the 0.01 / 0.1 / 1 s step strip; you can also type a time. Switch it off for just Set and Reset. Loop A-B (the repeat button after the Preview buttons) makes Play A-B repeat from A every time it reaches B, with the fade-in and fade-out each lap, so you can hear whether the section loops cleanly. It is for previewing only: it never loops the live program. On the player's own preview output the loop has no gap; on This browser there is a small gap at the wrap. Stop, switching Loop off or closing the window ends it. Smart Loop (the second tab) is not available yet.

On a phone the window fills the screen: A and B each have a card with − and + for the time and the fade length, Set A at playhead, Reset and Precision, one Nudge step control, and the Preview section with an Output menu (This browser unless you chose otherwise, so you hear it on the phone). Cancel and Save stay at the bottom; the trash button there is Remove trim (it removes A, B and the fades at once, like the button on a desktop; there is no confirmation, and you can set the points again).

Keyboard on the waveform (click it or Tab to it first; one marker, A, B or a trigger flag, is the selected one):

  • Left / Right nudge the selected marker by 0.1 s; hold Shift for 1 s or Alt for 0.01 s.
  • Up / Down choose the previous or next marker (A, B and the trigger flags in time order).
  • Delete (or Backspace) removes the selected trigger flag.
  • Drag the green A and red B markers and the purple trigger flags with the mouse; a click anywhere else moves the play position.

Cue

Click any playlist row to make it the cue: the track that is "up next". The Cue strip shows it with its playlist underneath; click the track's name (or the playlist name) to jump to that track in its playlist, where it flashes for a few seconds. Prev / Next move it along. Clear removes the cue so nothing is up next (the take and crossfade buttons then do nothing until you pick one). Companion's take and crossfade buttons use the cue too.

Play on Top

Use Play on Top on the Cue strip to play an announcement over the music: the live audio drops to 10% (settable in Settings), the cued track plays, and when it ends the music comes back up and carries on. The fade is 1.5 seconds each way, also settable. Open Ducking under the Cue strip to see the Reduction meter on the Input Gain row: a slim red bar that shows how far the music is turned down right now, from 0 (left) to -30 dB (right), with the number beside it (red while it ducks). Stay ducked until it ends is on the Ducking heading line.

Live input: duck when someone talks

The Live input card sits right under the Cue strip and Preview (it is a block of its own: you can move it in Edit layout; it is not shown on a phone-width page). It listens to one input channel (a Dante channel from the Dante USB adapter, or an analog interface) and turns the music down while someone talks, then brings it back. Choose the Device and the Channels (side by side) in the card (click Live input to open the setup; the chip, the level meter, Duck now and Solo are always visible) or in Settings → Audio, then switch Armed on (it starts off) with the switch in the Cue strip (see below) or in Settings. The card itself only says “armed” or “not armed”. The chip says Signal present, No signal or No input (with the reason), and the meter has a mark where ducking starts (see Live input: where ducking starts below). The ducking uses the same Ratio, Attack, Release and Stay ducked settings as Play on Top, and the same Reduction meter. Stay ducked is the same switch as in Play on Top: on, the dip is held for one second after the speech stops. Switching Armed or Duck now off uses the same quick 50 ms ramp. Duck now is a manual override: it holds the music down until you press it again, with or without an input and whether or not Armed is on (losing the input does not switch it off; a player restart does); Solo lets you listen to the input on the Preview output. This version is Duck only: the input is never mixed into the Live output. If the input disappears (a device unplugged, a stalled stream) the speech ducking ends within 100 ms (a quick 50 ms ramp, so there is no click) and the chip says No input (Detector stalled if the player's own detector stopped for a quarter of a second); it comes back by itself when the device returns.

Live input: where ducking starts

The Live input has its own threshold, separate from the Play on Top Threshold (a microphone and a playing track need different levels): the level, after the input gain, above which the music is turned down (−60 to 0 dB, in whole dB; for a stereo pair the louder channel counts). It is the mark on the meter, and the caption under the meter reads Duck above −35 dB. Drag the mark along the bars, or click (or press and drag) anywhere on the bars to move it there; or click the mark and use the arrow keys (1 dB, Shift 5 dB), Home and End; or use the Duck above field in Settings → Audio. The caption under the meter shows the value while you move it. Double-click the mark to go back to −30 dB. A change applies at once and is saved. While the level is above the mark and Armed is on, the mark turns red and the caption says Ducking, so you can set it by watching the meter. Ratio, Attack, Release and Stay ducked are still the Play on Top ones. A player that had no input threshold before starts with the Play on Top Threshold it was using, so nothing sounds different until you change it.

Live input: stereo pair, and the armed state in the Cue strip

The Channels menu offers Stereo pair choices first (Channel 1/Channel 2, Channel 2/Channel 3 …) and then every Single channel (Channel 1 (mono) …); a two-channel device has exactly three choices. With a pair the level meter has one bar for each channel (L and R) and the louder channel ducks, so one active microphone is enough; the input gain applies to both. Solo plays a pair as stereo on the Preview output and a single channel to both ears. Changing the choice restarts the capture for a moment: the chip says Switching… and the ducking is released with the quick ramp. A choice that the device cannot offer (a different device with fewer channels) falls back to the first valid one, with a note on the card.

While an input device is chosen, the Cue strip shows the armed state: with no cue, Input armed (green dot, which channels it listens to, a Signal present chip) or Input off under “No cue”; with a cue, a small pill at the top right (Input armed or Input off). Armed but no input shows an amber Input armed, no input with the reason. The switch there is the Armed setting itself: the card’s text, Settings and Companion follow it at once. The Input Gain slider under Ducking in the Cue strip is the level of the track played on top, not the Live input’s gain; the small i next to it says so.

RAM indicator

A track has to be decoded into RAM before it can play. A track that is in RAM starts instantly; one that is not waits a few seconds for the decode (about 5 seconds for a 15-minute MP3), so it is worth waiting for Ready before the show. The small indicator in the header, just left of Cache, shows a spinning ring (blue) with N/M and a small bar while the playlists are being decoded, a tick (green) with N/M when every track is in RAM, and an alert circle with Full (amber, and the Cache numbers turn amber too) when the cache is full and the oldest tracks are being dropped. N is the number of tracks in RAM, M the number of tracks used by the playlists. After loading it turns to the tick only when it has stayed ready for 2 seconds, so it does not flicker while the tracks around the cue are warmed. Hover it for the tooltip. The header reads left to right: indicator, Cache, RAM (the whole machine). A track row is marked only when it needs attention: a spinning blue ring means decoding now and a grey hollow ring means not loaded (starts after a short wait); no mark means it is in RAM and starts instantly. Each playlist says how many of its tracks are in RAM. If you press Go on a cue that is not in RAM yet, the button shows Loading… N s until the track starts: nothing is skipped or queued differently, it just tells you why there is a pause.

Playlist progress

Each playlist header shows where you are: Track N of M, the time played, the time left and the clock time the list will end. A chip for the playlist behavior shows at the right while the playlist is collapsed (expanded, the Playlist behavior menu already shows it). The bar has one segment per track, as wide as that track's real length: for a trimmed track that is its A to B window, so the time left is what will really play. Played tracks are dark green, the current one is outlined and filled to the position, and the tracks to come are empty (a very long playlist shows one continuous bar). In the rows, played tracks are dimmed and the playing track shows its loudness and elapsed / length under the name, with a thin line along the bottom; the seven buttons stay on one line. Stop at end and Next playlist count the time left to the end of the list (Next playlist also names the next one); Loop counts the current lap; Shuffle counts what is left of the current round; a track that loops forever says "looping this track". A playlist that is not playing only shows its totals.

The Settings tabs

Open Settings (the gear in the header). It has four tabs, and every control saves as soon as you change it: Look (Appearance: style, theme, show waveforms, pin the Live panel, the new-folder icon; Layout: Edit layout, Reset, Export and Import of the main-screen blocks), Audio (Audio: Preview output, Mirror, the Live input device, channels and Armed; Transitions: the default Go transition, the crossfade time and the fade-in at A and fade-out at B defaults), Companion (the address and OSC port, the variable prefix, Send test, the variables list) and System (Library: library folders, Rescan, file check; Health and alerts: the watchdog and push messages, see Status light and health; Maintenance: guides, ffmpeg, restart the player). The search box at the top finds a setting by its name.

Time warning

In Settings → Look → Time warnings (a row you can open and close; closed, it shows the colours) the big countdown changes colour as a track runs out, for example orange at 01:00 left and red at 00:30. It is always on. Change the times and colours, remove one with its ✕, or press Add a warning for more (there is no limit): they are named First warning, Warning 2, Warning 3, … and Final warning, longest time first. They are saved in the player, so every device, and the stage page, use the same ones.

Customising the layout

Settings → Look → Edit layout lets you rearrange the page: the Live, Cue, Preview, Library and Playlists blocks. Every block sits on a grid (twelve columns wide) that you can see while editing. Drag a block by the ⠿ handle in its orange bar to move it; it snaps to the grid, never overlaps another block (the one in the way is pushed down) and floats up to close gaps. Drag the orange handles on its right edge, bottom edge or corner to resize it. The buttons in its bar set it to ¼, ½, ¾ or full width, ↕ fits its height to its content (a block that is taller than its box scrolls inside it), the arrows move it one cell, and the eye hides a block you do not use (it fades out while you edit, and the eye brings it back). Esc cancels a drag. Buttons do nothing while you edit, so nothing can go on air by accident. Press Done to save. The layout is kept in this browser only, so each computer or tablet can have its own, and clearing the browser’s site data resets it. Reset layout returns to the standard one. It applies to wide screens; on a phone the page keeps its normal layout.

Moving a layout to another device: in Settings (or the bar at the bottom while editing) press Export layout, give it a name and it downloads a small file; tick Include appearance to carry the skin, theme and waveforms too. On the other device press Import layout and choose the file, drop it into the window, or paste its text (handy for sending it through a chat). You see a small preview first, and nothing changes until you press Apply. Undo import brings the previous layout back. A layout from a newer version of the player is refused; a damaged one is repaired. On a phone the import is saved and applies when the page is on a wider screen.

Mirror output

Switch Settings → Audio → Mirror live to a second device on (it is off by default) and a device menu appears under it, and a Mirror menu beside Output. It plays a copy of whatever is live on a second sound device: headphones, or a second Dante adapter. It follows the live track’s position, fades, ducking and volume, and it is separate from Preview. Choose Off to turn it off; the choice is remembered. Two separate adapters run on their own clocks and can drift slightly apart, so use it for monitoring or a backup feed, not for two speakers in the same room.

Output names

An output can have a nicer name, a group heading in the menus, or be hidden, without changing which device is used (the saved output stays the exact device name). The player starts with one built-in rule for the ALSA loopback card that Dante over IP uses: the first loopback entry reads Dante over IP (under Network audio, Live only) and the second is hidden. A hidden output is still listed while it is the one in use, and Settings → Audio → Show hidden outputs lists them dimmed. There is no editor yet: set the table with POST /api/settings/outputs, or by editing output_aliases in state.json while the player is stopped. Example: {"aliases": [{"match": "Dante USB I/O Module, USB Audio", "label": "Main PA", "group": "Dante", "hidden": false, "live_only": false}]}. match is the full device name as listed (capital letters count); null instead of the list brings back the built-in rule, and a saved list replaces it completely.

More file types, and file checks (ffmpeg)

wav, mp3, ogg and flac always work. If ffmpeg is installed on the player computer, you can also upload m4a, aac, aiff, caf, wma, opus, mp2, ac3 and the sound of video files (mp4, mov, mkv, webm). They are converted to FLAC at 48 kHz, 16-bit stereo: it is lossless like WAV but about half the size, and the original is not kept. WAV and FLAC files at other sample rates, higher bit depth or with more than two channels are converted the same way when uploaded. Without ffmpeg, those other formats are refused with a message. With ffmpeg these formats are converted when you upload them: 3gp, aac, ac3, aif, aifc, aiff, avi, caf, m4a, m4v, mkv, mov, mp2, mp4, oga, opus, webm, wma, wv. Settings → System → Maintenance shows whether ffmpeg was found.

ffmpeg also draws the waveforms, measures loudness and checks the library in the background. A yellow sign beside a track is a warning (for example more than two channels, or very short); a red sign means the file cannot be read or has no sound. Hover over the sign to read why, and over a track name to see its format. Settings → Check all files decodes every file to find damaged ones. ffmpeg runs one job at a time at low priority on at most two CPU cores, so it does not disturb playback.

Level match

Each playlist has a Level match menu. Match a track turns the louder tracks down to the level of the one you choose (marked REF); Fixed LUFS turns them down to a set loudness such as -18. It only ever turns tracks down, never up, and it does not affect Preview. A badge on each row shows the change in dB.

The Live card shows the live track's playlist under its title (click the title or the name to jump to it), a loop button, and the same dB bubble as the playlist row. Click the bubble to change the playlist's level match; in Fixed LUFS mode the + and − buttons raise or lower the target by 1 LUFS, which makes the live track louder or quieter.

Download from a link

The download button beside the search box saves the audio of a web link into the library, in a folder called Downloads. Paste the link, choose FLAC (the default: lossless, 48 kHz), MP3 or WAV, and press Download. Press Minimise while it works to get back to the player: a small box in the corner shows the progress, and when it is done it says so. Click it to jump to the new track in the library. A link to a playlist saves only its first track unless you tick Whole playlist. Only one download runs at a time. The player needs internet access for this, and you should only download things you have the right to use.

Pin the Live panel

The small pin at the top left of the Live panel keeps it in view. Pinned (a filled, upright pin; it is the default) the full panel scrolls away as usual and a one-line version stays at the top: the pin, LIVE, the title, pause (and stop on a computer), the time and the LIVE meters, with the PRV meters in a teal chip while you preview. Click the pin (or the one on the one-line panel) to unpin; it turns into an outlined, tilted pin. In Settings → Look you can pin or unpin, choose whether it shrinks to one line (off: the whole panel stays at the top) and whether this browser remembers the choice. On a phone the panel is pinned by default and the one-line version has two short rows.

Volume and output

The Vol slider changes the live level. Under the Live meters are M (mute), S (Solo live) and a speaker button: it opens the Output menu, which chooses which sound device the live audio uses. While you preview a track (or Solo live uses the preview output) a thinner Preview pair of meters, labelled PRV, appears to the right of the Live pair and shares its dB scale; under it is its own M, which mutes the preview output only (the live audio, the ducking and the Live meters are never touched), and a speaker button for the same kind of menu as the Preview panel's. The Preview panel has that menu too (headphone icon and the device name, to the right of its volume slider on a computer), to choose where the preview plays: this browser, the player's output, or both. It is a searchable menu: click it (or press Enter), type part of a name (spaces and punctuation are ignored, so dvs 1 finds the DVS Transmit pairs), use the arrow keys, Home and End to move, Enter to choose and Esc to close; System default and Off always stay at the top, and the list is read again each time it opens. Mirror and the preview output menus in Settings work the same way (changing it moves the playing track to the new device from the same point, with a brief gap while the device opens). The meter on the right shows the live level on a console-style scale from −inf to 0 dBFS (0 at the top; green, amber from −17 dBFS, red from −6 dBFS).

Playlist behavior and what a track does at its end

Each playlist has a Playlist behavior menu that says what happens when a track ends by itself: Stop, Loop Playlist (go on to the next track and start again at the top), Next Playlist (play to the end of the playlist, then start the next one) or Shuffle (every track once, in random order, before any repeats).

The green Play button in a playlist's header starts the playlist from its first track, exactly as if you had pressed Go on it, and the behavior then applies as normal. When the behavior is Shuffle the button reads Shuffle (with the crossing-arrows icon) and starts with a random track. It is disabled for an empty playlist or one whose files are all missing.

Each track also has one end-action button that you click to step through four settings:

The Live panel has the same button for the track on air. A track played with Play on Top plays once whatever its setting, apart from Loop forever. If the player restarts and resumes a track, a "loop once" track finishes the pass it was in and then carries on.

Moving the waveform on the live track jumps the audio that is on air, so be careful during a show.

Library & folders

  • Use the folder button beside the search box to create folders, and drag files into them. Dragging onto "Drop here to move to the top level" takes a file back out.
  • The pencil renames a file or folder; the red ✕ deletes it from the library.
  • Folder colours: click the small circle beside a folder and pick a colour. Every playlist row shows a coloured flag on its left edge in that folder's colour, so you can tell where a file lives at a glance. Click the flag and the folder lights up yellow in the library for four seconds.
  • The number next to a file is how many playlists use it, and its length is shown on the right.
  • Collapse all / Expand all: the two plain icon buttons in the Library header (chevron: open every folder; two inward arrows: close every folder) close or open every folder (hidden folders are skipped). Which folders are open is remembered on this device.
  • Favorites: the star on a folder moves it to a Favorites group at the top of the list, above All folders (both alphabetical). Click the star again to unfavorite it.
  • Hide a folder: the eye-off button takes a folder (and what is inside it) out of the library list, to tidy it. It only changes the list: the tracks stay in your playlists and play as before, and they are still found by the search. When something is hidden a Hidden button (a crossed-out eye with the number of hidden folders) appears in the Library header: switch it on to see the hidden folders (dimmed, with a hidden badge) and click the blue crossed-out eye on one to unhide it.
  • Search and hidden folders: if some matches are in hidden folders, a line says N tracks found in hidden folders · click to show. Click it to see just those folders (dimmed, each with its unhide eye); Hide these again or clearing the search puts them away.
  • Favorites and hidden folders are shared by every browser that opens the player; which folders are collapsed, and whether Hidden is on, are kept per device. Hidden folders are not offered in the track picker of a trigger point.

File information

Press the small i (a circle with an i) on a library row, or on a playlist track, to see the properties of that file. It opens next to the button, and closes with its ✕, the Esc key, a click or tap anywhere else, or by pressing the i again. Loudness figures take a few seconds to measure the first time and appear by themselves; they are remembered afterwards, and measured again only if the file changes. Hover over a value (or tap it) to read what it means.

  • Format and Codec: the kind of file (such as WAV or MP3) and how the sound is packed inside it. PCM means it is not compressed.
  • Duration: how long it plays. File size: the disk space it takes.
  • Bitrate: how much data is used for each second of sound. For MP3 and similar files, higher usually means better quality.
  • Sample rate: how many times a second the sound is measured. This player works at 48,000 Hz and converts other rates when the file loads.
  • Channels: mono is one channel, stereo is two (left and right). Bit depth: how finely each measurement is stored (16-bit is CD quality, 24-bit is studio quality).
  • LUFS: how loud the file sounds on average over its whole length. A number closer to zero is louder (-14 is louder than -23). This is what Level match uses.
  • True Peak (dBTP): the loudest point in the file. Above -1 dBTP can distort.
  • Loudness Range (LU): how much the loudness moves up and down. A small number is steady; a large one has quiet and loud parts.
  • Max Volume (dB): the loudest single moment of the sound. 0 dB is the loudest a file can go.
  • RMS (dB): the average level of the sound over the whole file. Lower means quieter overall.
  • Probe Score: how sure the file detector is about the format. It is not a quality score; an ordinary MP3 often scores about 50.

Most of these need ffmpeg. Without it the popover still shows the file size, the length and the loudness, and says "needs ffmpeg" for the rest. A file that cannot be read shows why.

Playlists

  • Several tracks at once: Ctrl/Cmd-click library tracks (Shift-click for a range), then drag them to a playlist, or use the Add to… menu that appears. Esc clears the selection.
  • Whole folder: drag a library folder onto a playlist to add all its tracks (sub-folders included). Drag a folder or tracks onto the dashed box next to the Create button, at the top of the Playlists column, to start a new playlist from them. A green confirmation appears at the bottom right; click it to jump to the new playlist (it also appears after Create).
  • Reorder tracks by dragging the ⣿ handle. Drag a track onto another playlist to move it there. Drag the handle at the top of a playlist to reorder playlists.
  • Rename a playlist with the Rename button or by double-clicking its name.
  • Removing a track from a playlist (✕) does not delete the file from the library.
  • Playlists, folders and colours are remembered. If the player restarts, it resumes the track where it left off.

Previewing & waveforms

The orange PVW (preview) button on a track lets you listen to a track privately before putting it on air. It plays through a separate preview output (the headphone jack) or through this browser, whichever you choose in Settings → Preview. Press the button again to pause it.

While something is previewed, the File information strip above the waveform shows its length, format, bitrate, sample rate, channels, loudness (LUFS) and peak on one line (the peak turns amber above -1 dB; a value still being worked out shows a pulsing dot and analyzing…). More opens every value as small cards (including loudness range, max volume and RMS) and Hide folds it back; the choice is remembered. It follows whichever file you preview, from the library, the trim window or a playlist row. Under a playlist row it is a single compact line with an All details… link that opens the full information window. Values still being measured say "measuring…" and fill in by themselves; without ffmpeg you see only what can be read without it.

Turn on Settings → Look → Show waveforms to see the shape of the live and cued tracks. Click or drag on a waveform to jump through the track, like a seek bar.

Status light and health

The dot beside the title shows the player’s health. The shape matters as well as the colour: Orange ring (pulsing): starting up and checking the library. Green circle: ready. Amber triangle: degraded: something needs attention but the audio still works (a missing Preview output, low disk space, Companion not answering). Red diamond: a problem (for example the live output is not available). Hover over it to read the reason.

Click the dot to open the health panel: seven checks, each with an icon, its name and, when it is not OK, one sentence saying what is wrong and what to do: Live output, Preview output, Audio engine, Companion (it is “not known yet” until Companion has asked the player for something once; after that a silence of more than 30 seconds is a warning), Media drive space (a warning below 10 % free or 5 GB, a problem below 1 GB; the row reads like 2.0 GB free (2 %), or less than 1 % when there is a little space left), Track cache and Player uptime. Copy report copies a plain-text report to paste into a chat or an email when you ask for help, so the person helping sees the same facts you do: the version, the state, every check with its sentence, the audio driver the sound system selected (for example alsa; not detected until the first audio device has been opened), the Live and Preview device names, how often the watchdog has restarted the audio engine, whether push messages are on, Process memory (what the player is using, in MB) and Open files (how many files the player holds open; Linux only, otherwise not available), the date and the uptime. The last two lines are only there for watching a long run: they never change a check or the dot. The report contains no addresses and no file paths. Open Variables opens the Companion variables page. Press Esc to close the panel; the focus goes back to the dot.

The watchdog (Settings → System → Health and alerts, on by default) restarts the audio engine only if the output stops moving for 10 seconds while something is playing; the live track carries on from where it was. It never restarts the service. It restarts at most 3 times in 10 minutes; after that it leaves the Audio engine check red so that you see it. Player name (same place) is the name used in push messages, the test message and the report; empty means the machine’s host name, and the line under the field shows the name in use. The Audio engine row always says how many times the watchdog has restarted the engine since the player started. While a track plays the player does not ask the sound system for its device list (that can disturb a running stream): the Live output is checked from the sound card list instead, and the full device list is read again when nothing plays. Amber for a missing Preview output decides whether a missing Preview device makes the dot amber. On Linux the Live output is recognised by the sound card’s name (its id, such as Module), not by its number, so unplugging and replugging the adapter is noticed and a card that comes back under another number is still found. If two identical adapters are plugged in and the first one is taken away, the player cannot tell whether the one left is the same device: it does not raise a problem for that, the audio engine check still watches the output, and the log has a line about it.

Push on state change (same place, off by default) sends a message to a webhook (n8n, Slack, Discord or your own) when a problem starts, when it recovers, when degraded lasts more than 5 minutes, and when the watchdog restarts the audio engine (tick the ones you want). A state has to hold for 5 seconds before it is sent, so a flapping device does not flood the channel. Hold messages until nothing is playing collects them while a track plays and sends them as one message afterwards. Send a test message sends one at once and shows a green chip with the time, or a red one with the reason (for example Timed out or Refused (HTTP 404)). Only the host of the address is ever shown; the rest of it is kept private. If a message cannot be sent, the panel says Last push failed with the reason.

In Companion: the variables health_state (starting, ready, degraded, problem) and health_text (a short reason), the feedback Player health and the preset Health (amber when degraded, red on a problem) need module 2.8.0.

Using Companion

In Companion, add buttons that send commands to the player. The most useful ones:

Arming the Live input from Companion: the Input armed toggle preset (in the Live input category) is a green button with white INPUT / UNARMED when the input is not armed, and a red one with white INPUT / ARMED when it is. Press it to toggle: it is the same Armed setting as the switch in the player’s Cue container (where you arm it on the player) and in Settings, and it follows within one poll. The action is Input arm / disarm (arm, disarm or toggle), the feedback is Input armed, and the variable mp_input_armed is 1 (armed) or 0 (not armed). This needs the Companion module 2.7.10 or newer.

The player also sends information back to Companion (what is playing, time left, levels, status) so your buttons can show it. Settings → Companion → View lists everything available. Variable prefix (same tab) is the start of every variable name (the default is mp_); changing it asks first, because buttons and triggers that use the old names must then be updated. Send test (same tab) sends a test value, mp_test, to the address in use, so you can check that it reaches Companion; OSC gives no reply, so look for it in Companion’s variables. If the address is a name that cannot be found right now (for example offline), it is still saved, a note says so, and the player keeps trying.

Stage page

A plain page that shows the live track's time remaining in big digits, for a stage display. It works in any browser, and in a web element of another program such as a FreeShow stage layout. It only shows; it has no buttons. Open it at http://<player>:8080/stage.

  • /stage/a is the time only, /stage/b adds the track name above it, /stage/c adds the name and a thin progress bar along the bottom (how far through the A to B part of the track it is).
  • Options after the address: ?name=1 (track name), ?bar=1 (progress bar), ?bg=transparent (default is black). They also work on top of /stage/a, b and c, for example /stage/b?bar=1.
  • The time is mm:ss, and h:mm:ss from one hour up (59:59, then 1:00:00). The digits fill whatever space the element has.
  • Paused: the time dims and PAUSED shows below it. Nothing playing, or the player not answering for about 3 seconds: a dim --:--. It reconnects by itself.
  • The colours come from Settings → Time warnings (every warning time and colour you have set), exactly like the Live countdown; the bar uses them too.
For a transparent background the page that embeds it must not set a dark color-scheme, or the browser paints the frame solid. Use bg=black if in doubt.

If something is wrong

  • Light is red: hover over it. Common causes are a disconnected audio device or a track that could not load.
  • No sound: check the speaker button (Output menu) under the Live meters in the Live panel, and the M (mute) button next to it.
  • Troubleshooting: output device missing: the device must be running before it can be chosen. With Dante Virtual Soundcard, start DVS first (it lists its channels as stereo pairs named DVS Transmit N-N, for example DVS Transmit 1-2). Check that Windows lists it under Sound settings, then press Settings → Refresh devices (the lists also refresh when you open Settings or an Output menu). A yellow note under the Output menu says when a saved device was not found; the player then uses the system default until you choose the device again.
  • Lengths missing: they are measured in the background after new files are added; give it a minute.
  • Added files directly on the machine: use Settings → System → Rescan.
  • Player acting strangely: Settings → System → Restart player restarts it in a few seconds. A playing track carries on from where it was.
  • Companion variables empty: check the address and port under Settings → Companion. An incomplete address (for example 192.168.1.) is not used: the player keeps the last good one and says so.
